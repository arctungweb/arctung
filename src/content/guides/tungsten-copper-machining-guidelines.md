Tungsten copper has a reputation for being difficult to machine. Mostly, that reputation belongs to pure tungsten — sintered, hard, and genuinely abrasive. Infiltrated W/Cu is a different material to cut: a tungsten skeleton filled with copper, and it behaves closer to a hard bronze than to a refractory metal. This guide covers what shops need to know before putting W/Cu on a lathe or a machining center.

## What the composite cuts like

Infiltrated W/Cu is a two-phase material, and that is the key to everything about machining it. The copper phase cuts easily; the tungsten skeleton is what limits your speeds and dulls your tools. Practical consequences:

- **Carbide tooling throughout.** HSS works briefly and then does not; coated carbide is the economical default for turning, milling and drilling.
- **Positive rake angles.** The copper phase tears rather than shears cleanly if you push it with a negative geometry — sharp, positively raked edges leave a better surface.
- **Flood coolant.** Heat that stays in the cut smears copper over the tungsten skeleton and glazes the surface. Coolant keeps the chip clear and the finish honest.
- **No grinding required** for ordinary turning, milling or drilling. Surface grinding is reserved for faces that need it dimensionally, not because the material demands it.

Two habits separate good W/Cu work from scrapped blanks: take light finishing passes so the copper phase is not torn out of the surface, and support slender sections properly. The composite is stiff, but it chatters on thin, poorly clamped work just like any dense metal.

## Threading W/Cu

Cutting a clean thread in tungsten copper is a quality-control problem more than a machining problem. The copper phase tears easily, and a torn thread root becomes a stress riser exactly where vibration concentrates — a welding gun shank, a threaded electrode stud, a fixture bolt.

Practical threading guidance:

- Cut threads on a conventional lathe with sharp carbide inserts; single-point threading gives the cleanest roots in the composite.
- Gauge every batch with go/no-go thread gauges — a thread that gauges true on arrival is the only thread that screws into your holder the first time.
- Metric and UNC/UNF forms are routine; special forms, left-hand threads and tapered shanks are all cut to drawing.
- Match the thread fit class your mating holder was designed around. In welding guns the threaded joint carries current, force and tip-change release all at once, so fit class is not cosmetic.

If your consumption shows thread damage rather than face wear, the fix is usually seating practice or holder design — not the alloy.

## Drilling and small holes

Drilling behaves like a free-machining bronze at reduced surface speed. Use sharp carbide drills, peck on deeper holes to clear chips, and expect accurate, clean bores at typical workshop feeds. Precision bores — the H7 wheel bores that have to drop onto an existing shaft — are finish-bored or reamed rather than drilled to size, which is standard practice for any metal but worth stating: bore tolerance lives or dies on the finishing operation, not the drill.

## When to machine it yourself — and when not to

Buying rods and blocks and machining in-house makes sense when you cut caps, shanks and simple facings on short notice and your machine time is cheap. Stock shapes in both W/Cu 75/25 and 70/30, rough-machined or ground, exist exactly for that workflow.

But run the numbers honestly for finished parts. A wheel with a keyed bore, a bevel profile and a runout requirement needs tooling, setup, gauging and balancing — and dense W/Cu punishes packaging and handling mistakes in transit. For anything beyond simple facings, finished parts to drawing often cost less than stock plus your shop time, and they arrive with dimensional records.
