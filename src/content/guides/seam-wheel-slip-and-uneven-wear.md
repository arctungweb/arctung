A tungsten-copper seam wheel that wears evenly is doing its job; replace it on the cycle described in our [wear and reorder guide](/guides/seam-welding-wheel-wear-and-reorder/) and forget about it. The wheels that cause trouble are the ones wearing *unevenly* — one flank grooved while the other looks new, the face skinned, the OD gone out-of-round. Maintenance teams often blame the wheel material. Almost always, the wheel is the messenger: uneven wear is how a seam welder reports mechanical and electrical faults elsewhere. This guide maps each abnormal wear pattern to its cause and fix.

## Normal wear versus abnormal wear

Normal wear on a properly run wheel is unremarkable: the contact face consumes slowly and evenly, the profile stays true, the seam width holds. The abnormal patterns — and each has a distinct cause — are:

- **Single-side (flank) wear** — one edge of the face consumed, the other intact.
- **Local grooving** — a circumferential groove worn into an otherwise sound face.
- **Skinned or torn faces** — copper smeared, lifted or ripped from the surface.
- **Edge chipping and out-of-round wheels** — mechanical shock leaving the wheel lumpy.

Chinese maintenance literature is blunt about it: most field wheel consumption is abnormal wear, not service life — and abnormal wear is fixable.

## Slip: when rolling becomes sliding

A seam wheel is designed to *roll* along the strip. The moment it slips, rolling friction becomes sliding friction, and sliding wears a hard composite like a grinding wheel wears steel — scratches first, then grooves, then chatter in the seam. The usual culprits, in field order:

1. **Travel speed mismatch.** The wheel surface speed and the strip feed speed are not synchronized, so the contact drags. Check drive belts, gear mesh and friction-drive surfaces for wear or looseness.
2. **Workpiece feed jitter or wander.** A strip that surges or snatches through the machine forces the wheel to slip and catch. Fix the feed mechanism before touching the wheel.
3. **Bearing clearance and hub drag.** A wheel that does not spin freely skids across the work.

Slip also shows up electrically: a dragging wheel intermittently changes contact conditions, and seam quality wanders for reasons that look like schedule drift but are mechanical.

## A special case: the burning hub

On machines where the wheel runs on a tapered sleeve or conductor shaft, Chinese maintenance records describe a failure mode worth knowing: copper chips and degraded lubricant pack between the shaft and the sleeve. Contact area drops, resistance climbs, and welding current now generates heat inside the hub assembly instead of at the seam — until the assembly itself burns. The countermeasures are unglamorous but effective: strip and re-lap the taper on a fixed interval (roughly quarterly on high-utilization lines, per those sources), flush chips and old grease out with compressed air before re-greasing, and keep cooling waterways descaled. If your wheels run hot at the *bore* rather than the face, look here first.

## Misalignment: the cause of single-side wear

Upper and lower wheels that are not parallel — or a welding head drifted off vertical, or worn bearings — concentrate the entire electrode force on one edge of the contact. The result is the classic single-side wear pattern, plus seam width that varies along the weld and seals that leak intermittently.

The fix is a geometry check, not a new wheel: verify wheel parallelism and head perpendicularity with a dial indicator, and replace worn bearings. Confirm electrode force while you are at it — Chinese troubleshooting sources list **excessive force** as a leading cause of rapid groove wear, because operators fighting false welds turn pressure up until the softest thing in the stack starts collapsing.

## Heat, cooling and contamination

Three process-side causes round out the list:

- **Cooling failure.** Blocked waterways, scale, or warm coolant let the face run hot until even a W/Cu face starts to skin and tear. Check flow daily; descale on schedule.
- **Excessive heat input.** Current too high or travel too slow keeps the contact at eroding temperatures; on galvanized strip, vaporizing zinc accelerates pickup and sticking.
- **Dirty strip.** Mill scale, swarf, burrs and oil act as abrasive paste between face and work — continuous mechanical wear no alloy can outlast. Clean the seam track before the wheel sees it; a knurled face helps keep the track clean where strip condition cannot be improved.

## When the wheel itself is done

Dress lightly and early — aggressive grinding of a surface that only needs truing throws wheel life away. Track circularity: Chinese maintenance guidance suggests redressing once runout grows past roughly 0.05 mm; our wheels are machined to 0.02 mm runout new, so anything approaching that figure is measurable drift. When dressing no longer restores the profile, or the bore shows the hub problems above, reorder rather than nurse — with a 10–14 day lead time and no minimum order, a marginal wheel costs more to keep than to replace.
