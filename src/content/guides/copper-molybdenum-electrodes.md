"Copper molybdenum electrode" is a search that usually means one of two things: a buyer who wants CuMo specifically, or a buyer who wants a copper-refractory electrode and is not yet sure which refractory. Both are worth answering honestly, because choosing between copper molybdenum and copper tungsten changes electrode cost, wear life and the geometry you can hold — and the two are routinely quoted against each other as if they were substitutes.

They are not. They are siblings with different centres of gravity.

## What copper molybdenum actually is

CuMo is made the same way as CuW: a pressed and sintered molybdenum skeleton, vacuum-infiltrated with copper to fill the remaining porosity. The copper phase carries current and flushes heat; the molybdenum skeleton holds shape and resists the arc. Standard grades run from roughly 50/50 to 70/30 Mo/Cu by weight, with 70/30 and 60/40 doing most of the industrial work.

The consequences of swapping tungsten for molybdenum follow directly from the two metals:

- **Molybdenum is roughly half the density of tungsten** (10.2 vs 19.3 g/cm³). A finished CuMo electrode weighs about two-thirds what the same electrode weighs in CuW — less mass on the spindle, less on the holder, and less material cost per part.
- **Molybdenum melts and softens lower** (2623 °C vs 3422 °C). Both are refractory by any ordinary standard, but under severe discharge the margin is smaller.
- **Molybdenum is cheaper as a commodity**, and CuMo inherits that.
- **Molybdenum has a higher thermal expansion** than tungsten, so CuMo moves more per degree than an equivalent W/Cu grade.

## The comparison that matters

| Property (typical) | CuMo 70/30 | W/Cu 75/25 | W/Cu 70/30 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Density | ≈ 10.0 g/cm³ | ≈ 15.2 g/cm³ | ≈ 14.5 g/cm³ |
| Electrical conductivity | ≈ 45–50% IACS | ≥ 38% IACS | ≥ 42% IACS |
| Thermal expansion (CTE) | ≈ 9–11 µm/m·K | ≈ 8.5–9.5 µm/m·K | ≈ 10–11 µm/m·K |
| Skeleton melting point | 2623 °C | 3422 °C | 3422 °C |
| Wear resistance in EDM | Good | Excellent | Excellent |
| Machinability | Conventional | Conventional | Conventional |
| Relative material cost | Lower | Higher | Higher |

Read the table as a trade, not a ranking: CuMo gives you conductivity and cost, W/Cu gives you density, wear resistance and thermal stability.

## Where CuMo is the better electrode

- **Large, simple cavities at high current.** When the burn is roughing and the electrode is big, wear rate is dominated by thermal load per unit area rather than by fine-feature retention. CuMo's higher conductivity and lower mass help here, and the cost difference on a large electrode is real money.
- **Electrodes you will consume quickly.** If a job eats several electrodes per cavity regardless of material, paying for tungsten's wear resistance is paying for life you will not use.
- **Weight-limited setups.** Small EDM machines, light heads and manual tooling changeover all care about electrode mass. A 30% weight cut per electrode is a handling improvement, not just a cost one.
- **Resistance welding and thermal spreaders** where the duty is conduction rather than abrasion.

## Where W/Cu is the better electrode

- **Fine ribs, knife edges and sharp corners.** The tungsten skeleton holds detail through a finishing burn; a molybdenum skeleton at the same copper content rounds over sooner on thin sections.
- **Long finishing sequences** where electrode wear per cavity is the number in your quote. Low, even wear means fewer electrodes per job, and that usually beats the material saving.
- **Low-CTE joints.** When the electrode or the part it mounts to is thermally matched, tungsten's lower expansion is the safer partner.
- **High-density requirements** — seismic counterweights, radiation shielding, vibration damping — where CuMo simply cannot reach the density.
- **Severe discharge conditions** where the margin between 2623 °C and 3422 °C is doing real work.

## The mistake to avoid

The common error is specifying by habit rather than by failure mode. If your electrodes are failing by **corner rounding and dimensional drift**, you want tungsten. If they are failing by **thermal erosion at high current, or by cost per cavity on a simple burn**, CuMo is worth quoting. If they are failing by **cracking at the holder**, neither material is the answer — the fixture is.

Ask which failure you are actually paying for before you ask which alloy is cheaper.
