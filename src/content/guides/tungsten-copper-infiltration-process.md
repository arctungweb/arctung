Tungsten copper cannot be cast. The two metals are practically insoluble in each other in the solid state, form no intermediate compounds, and their melting points are almost 2,600 °C apart — so there is no alloy melt to pour. Every tungsten-copper electrode in existence is a powder-metallurgy product, and the route that dominates industrial production of the common electrode grades is **skeleton sintering plus copper infiltration**. Understanding that route explains most of what separates a good CuW billet from a mediocre one, and it is the process behind every wheel and rod we ship.

## Why infiltration, specifically

For tungsten contents above roughly 60%, Chinese industry sources describe infiltration as effectively the only viable production route: direct liquid-phase sintering of mixed W and Cu powders struggles to densify copper-poor compositions. The trick is to stop trying to consolidate both metals at once. Instead:

1. **Press** pure tungsten powder into a shape with deliberately connected porosity.
2. **Sinter** that skeleton until the tungsten particles weld to each other — strong enough to survive handling, porous enough to drink molten copper.
3. **Place** the skeleton against a weighed copper block in graphite tooling and heat everything, in hydrogen or vacuum, past the melting point of copper (practical temperatures run roughly 1,100–1,400 °C).
4. **Let capillary pressure** pull the melt through the connected pore network until the skeleton is full.

The result is a near-fully-dense two-phase composite: a rigid tungsten matrix with copper filling it like water in a sponge. No alloying, no intermetallics.

## The parameters that decide the outcome

**Skeleton relative density** is the master variable. It fixes how much copper the billet takes — and with it the composition, conductivity and hardness. Chinese batch-stability studies of infiltrated W-Cu treat skeleton density as a designed value held to within about half a percent across powder lots, because drift in the skeleton drifts every property downstream.

**Tungsten powder grain size** sets the sintering behavior: coarser powder needs a higher sintering temperature to reach the same skeleton density, so powder grade and furnace schedule have to be qualified together, not chosen independently.

**Infiltration temperature and wetting.** Molten copper wets tungsten well — near-perfectly at the upper end of the infiltration range — and capillary force does the filling work. Too low a temperature leaves the melt sluggish and pores unfilled; too high, and the copper's surface tension falls until it bleeds back out of the skeleton, while trapped gas in closed pores expands and disrupts the structure.

**Copper charge weight.** Charging slightly more copper than the theoretical pore volume ensures the skeleton fills completely rather than starving at the far end.

## Where the defects come from

Residual porosity is the signature defect of a badly run infiltration line, and it has three known causes worth knowing as a buyer:

- **Closed pores in the skeleton.** Once the sintered skeleton passes roughly 80% relative density, pores begin to close off and copper can no longer reach them. That is why electrode-grade skeletons are designed below that threshold.
- **Solidification shrinkage.** Copper shrinks as it freezes inside a skeleton with a very different thermal expansion. The last metal to solidify can leave shrinkage cavities, typically toward the ingate side.
- **Poor wetting in fine pores.** Where capillary radius gets small, wetting force may not beat the melt's own constraints, leaving un-infiltrated spots.

Porosity is not cosmetic: it shows up in service as lowered conductivity, premature face erosion on a welding wheel, and arcing damage on an EDM electrode.

## What a buyer should actually check

The checks are simple because the failure mechanisms are simple:

- **Density by displacement.** Weigh the part, measure its volume, compare with the grade's published density. Porosity reads light; nothing else on the floor catches a poorly infiltrated billet faster. Our W/Cu 75/25 runs ≈ 15.2 g/cm³ typical and 70/30 ≈ 14.5 g/cm³.
- **Composition against the grade.** W 75% ±2% or 70% ±2%, balance copper — on the mill certificate, not just the quotation.
- **Internal soundness.** Ultrasonic inspection is standard practice in Chinese high-reliability W-Cu production precisely because infiltration defects are internal. For critical wheels, ask whether the batch was tested.
- **Batch consistency.** Ask how the supplier controls incoming tungsten powder. Batch-to-batch drift in CuW almost always starts at the powder lot, which is why mature producers qualify powder suppliers, not just furnace schedules.
