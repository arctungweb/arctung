A projection welding electrode or die facing is purchased for a specific fixture, fastener, sheet stack, and weld schedule. An RFQ that says only “tungsten copper electrode” leaves the supplier to guess the working face, mounting interface, cooling passage, and inspection points—the features that determine whether the part fits and can be reordered consistently.

This guide turns the requirement into a quote-ready package for W/Cu 75/25 or W/Cu 70/30 components. It covers finished electrodes, die facings, near-net blanks, and W/Cu faces used with cooled holders. For the broader application context, see the [resistance welding electrodes page](/applications/resistance-welding-electrodes/).

## 1. Define the part's job in the assembly

Begin by stating whether the requested item is:

- a finished projection welding electrode;
- a flat or domed die facing;
- a cap, shank, holder, or fixture component;
- a W/Cu face used with a cooled shank or holder;
- a rod, block, or near-net blank that your shop will finish; or
- a complete custom component supplied to the drawing.

This distinction controls what the supplier must quote. A shop blank needs stock dimensions, machining allowance, and delivery condition. A finished electrode needs every functional surface and interface defined. A custom assembly also needs enough section detail to show how the W/Cu portion relates to the holder and cooling features.

W/Cu is typically used at the working face where resistance to deformation, coating pickup, or gradual wear matters. The surrounding holder or die body may use another material when its main jobs are structural support and current delivery. The [resistance welding alloy comparison](/guides/resistance-welding-electrode-alloys-overview/) explains that material boundary in more detail.

## 2. Draw the working face explicitly

Projection welding tools may use flat, domed, radiused, or otherwise profiled working faces. Do not identify the shape by name alone. Put the controlling dimensions on the drawing, including where applicable:

- face diameter, width, radius, or dome height;
- relationship between the face and the projection or workpiece;
- overall electrode or insert length;
- shoulder, step, or seating dimensions;
- permitted edge breaks;
- datum used to locate the face;
- concentricity or runout where alignment is functionally important; and
- the dimensions that may change during dressing.

A face can be machined to the requested shape only when the shape is measurable. Notes such as “standard projection face” are usually insufficient because the matching fastener, fixture, and validated process may not be standard.

Distinguish the original manufacturing dimension from the service limit used by your plant. The supplier can inspect a new part against the drawing, while the buyer should control any dressing or replacement limit derived from the actual welding process.

## 3. Specify the mounting and seating interface

The working face must return to the same position when the consumable is replaced. Show how the part mounts and which surface establishes its location. The RFQ may need:

- a threaded connection, including form, size, pitch, direction, and fit class;
- a straight or tapered shank;
- a shoulder or face used as the seating datum;
- an internal bore, counterbore, or locating diameter;
- wrench flats, keyways, slots, or removal features;
- overall installed length or stick-out; and
- the relationship between the mounting axis and working face.

If the part is threaded, use the complete callout rather than supplying only a nominal diameter. The [threaded tungsten copper electrode guide](/guides/threaded-tungsten-copper-electrode-specification/) provides the full interface checklist.

When replacing an existing component, send the approved drawing revision if it exists. If it does not, a sample plus measured dimensions and clear photographs can support review, but critical interfaces should still be converted into a controlled drawing before repeat production.

## 4. Show cooling passages in section

Internal cooling should be treated as part geometry, not as a general note. If the electrode, facing, or holder contains a water passage, provide a section view and dimension the relevant features:

- passage or bore diameter;
- depth and endpoint location;
- remaining wall thickness near the working face;
- counterbores and transitions;
- alignment with the mating holder or water circuit; and
- geometry around the intended sealing or connection location.

Tungsten copper can conduct heat away from the working face, but the benefit depends on the holder and water circuit actually removing that heat. A generic note such as “cooling hole as standard” does not tell the machine shop where the passage must end or how it must align with the existing fixture.

Do not add an assumed flow rate, pressure, temperature, or universal face-to-passage distance unless it comes from the validated machine design. Those values depend on the holder, circuit, duty cycle, and welding process and should be provided by the equipment or process owner.

## 5. Choose the grade from the limiting problem

ArcTung supplies W/Cu 75/25 and W/Cu 70/30. Both grades can be machined into electrodes, die facings, rods, blocks, and custom parts, but they balance wear resistance and conductivity differently.

- **W/Cu 75/25** is the harder grade. It is generally the starting direction when face deformation, mushrooming, or loss of working geometry limits service life.
- **W/Cu 70/30** is the higher-conductivity grade. It is generally the starting direction when the projection welding schedule is more constrained by current delivery and heat removal.

These are selection directions, not automatic acceptance rules. If a fixture and weld schedule have already been validated with one grade, retain that grade on the drawing and purchase order unless the process owner can run a controlled trial. Changing the alloy without reviewing the schedule may alter both electrode heating and wear behavior.

Use the [W75Cu25 versus W70Cu30 guide](/guides/w75cu25-vs-w70cu30/) when the RFQ needs a documented grade decision. If a numerical material property is an acceptance requirement, state that value and its test condition rather than assuming the grade name alone defines the limit.

## 6. Decide between stock, a near-net blank, and a finished part

There are three practical purchasing routes:

1. **Rod or rectangular block.** Appropriate when your own shop will machine the complete electrode or die facing.
2. **Near-net blank.** Appropriate when the supplier can remove bulk material while your shop retains the final fixture-specific operations.
3. **Finished custom component.** Appropriate when the part contains related faces, bores, threads, profiles, or cooling features that should be produced and inspected from one drawing.

ArcTung lists W/Cu 75/25 and W/Cu 70/30 rods, blocks, and near-net blanks as available forms. Stock can be supplied rough-machined or ground on request. Ground stock can provide a cleaner reference surface when the machining plan needs one, while rough-machined stock may be sufficient when every surface will be cut again.

For a finished component, identify the complete turning, milling, drilling, threading, or surface-grinding scope. Keep stock dimensions, machining allowance, and final dimensions separate so the quote does not confuse the blank envelope with the acceptance drawing.

Compare the available routes on the [electrode rods and blocks page](/products/rods-blocks/) and the [custom tungsten copper parts page](/products/custom-parts/).

## 7. Put required inspection records on the order

Ask for records that correspond to the risks in the actual part. A practical package may include:

- alloy identification;
- batch traceability;
- composition, density, or hardness records when required by the buyer's quality system;
- dimensional inspection against the named drawing revision;
- working-face dimensions;
- mounting and seating dimensions;
- thread-gauge results for threaded parts; and
- cooling-passage dimensions where they are drawing-critical.

Do not request a generic certificate bundle without stating how receiving inspection will use it. Requirements added after machining may not be available in the form your quality system expects. Put the requested records and any buyer-defined acceptance limits on the RFQ before quotation.

The [incoming inspection checklist](/guides/wcu-electrode-quality-checklist/) can be used to build the receiving plan for the first article and repeat batches.

## 8. Control the repeat order

A projection welding consumable becomes easier to buy after the first approved part only if the definition remains controlled. Keep the same:

- part number and drawing revision;
- W/Cu grade;
- working-face geometry;
- mounting and seating interface;
- cooling geometry;
- critical dimensions and inspection method; and
- required batch or dimensional records.

When a process change requires a new face, grade, or cooling feature, release a new revision rather than changing the purchase-order text while reusing the old drawing. This gives the supplier and receiving team one source of truth and reduces the risk of an obsolete machining program being used for a repeat batch.

## Lead-time and quantity planning

Current product data lists typical lead times of 10–14 days for standard stock shapes and 20–25 days for custom tooling and machining. Stock sizes are listed with no minimum order, while custom-part quantity is quoted for the specific component.

Treat those figures as planning references rather than shipment promises. Confirm the current schedule against the drawing, quantity, inspection scope, and destination in the supplier's quotation.

## Copy-and-paste RFQ checklist

Send the following information for a projection welding electrode or die facing:

1. Part function and welding application
2. W/Cu grade: 75/25 or 70/30
3. Finished component, near-net blank, rod, or block
4. Working-face shape and controlling dimensions
5. Mounting, seating, and datum features
6. Overall installed length or stick-out
7. Cooling-passage section and dimensions, if applicable
8. Stock envelope and machining allowance, if buying a blank
9. Final drawing, revision, units, and 3D file when available
10. Quantity for the first article and expected repeat batch
11. Critical dimensional inspection points
12. Required alloy, traceability, and batch records
13. Delivery destination and receiving restrictions

A complete package lets the supplier quote the material, machining, and inspection scope without inventing missing geometry. Send the checklist and drawing through the [contact page](/contact/) for review before releasing the order.
