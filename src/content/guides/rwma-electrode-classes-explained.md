If you buy resistance welding electrodes in North America, sooner or later a drawing or a quote says "RWMA Class 11" — and if you have never had the system explained, the number tells you nothing. The classification is genuinely useful once decoded: it sorts electrode materials by family and duty, so that a buyer in one plant and a supplier on another continent mean the same metal. This guide walks through the system and shows exactly where tungsten copper sits.

## What RWMA classes are

The Resistance Welding Manufacturers Alliance (formerly the Resistance Welder Manufacturers' Association, hence "RWMA") publishes a classification of electrode and holder materials. The classes group materials into families, roughly in order of increasing strength and decreasing conductivity:

- **Classes 1–3** — wrought copper alloys for general electrode duty, from high-conductivity work through harder, stronger grades.
- **Classes 4 and up through the copper-alloy range** — increasingly specialized strengthened coppers for holders, fixtures and high-strength duty.
- **Classes 10–12** — refractory composites, dominated by tungsten copper and related sintered materials.
- **Class 13** — pure tungsten.
- **Class 14** — pure molybdenum.

Two cautions before the details. First, the class number specifies a **family and nominal composition range, not a certified property set** — two suppliers' Class 10 material can differ in density and hardness within tolerance. Second, class designations describe the **material**, not the electrode shape or the schedule.

## The refractory classes in detail

This is where tungsten copper lives, and where buyers most often need precision:

| RWMA class | Material family | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Class 10 | Tungsten copper composite (roughly CuW70-type, ~70% W) | Flash and upset welding dies, high-wear facings |
| Class 11 | Tungsten copper composite (roughly CuW75-type, ~75% W) | Seam wheels, high-force facings, projection dies |
| Class 12 | Higher-tungsten refractory composite | Specialized erosion-resistant duty |
| Class 13 | Pure tungsten | Extreme-heat inserts, cross-wire welding facings |
| Class 14 | Pure molybdenum | Flash welding dies, specialized high-temp duty |

The useful pattern: from Class 10 to 13, tungsten content and wear resistance rise while conductivity falls. Class 10 is the more conductive, faster-dressing end of the CuW range; Class 11 is the harder, more wear-resistant end that holds profile longer. That is precisely the [W70Cu30 vs W75Cu25 trade-off](/guides/w75cu25-vs-w70cu30/) described elsewhere on this site.

A note on precision: supplier literature occasionally assigns nominal percentages to these classes with more confidence than the standard itself does. Treat "Class 10 ≈ 70% W–Cu" and "Class 11 ≈ 75% W–Cu" as the practical mapping the industry actually trades on — and always confirm the **actual composition and properties on the mill certificate**, which is what your quality system will audit anyway.

## How to use classes as a buyer

1. **Quote in both languages.** Put the class designation on the RFQ ("RWMA Class 11 equivalent, W/Cu 75/25") — the class for your quality system, the composition for the mill.
2. **Never let the class substitute for properties.** If your process depends on conductivity or hardness limits, specify the numbers, not just the class.
3. **Check certificates, not catalogues.** Reputable suppliers ship with material test reports; third-party stamped reports to ASTM B702 are available on our material on request.
4. **Match the class to the failure mode.** Heat-limited stations want the more conductive end; wear-limited stations want the harder end. Forging the class number onto the drawing without that analysis is how lines end up with the wrong member of the family.

## Common questions

**Is a higher RWMA class always better?** No. Higher refractory classes trade conductivity for wear. The right class is the one matched to your failure mode, not the highest number.

**Do EU and Asian suppliers use RWMA classes?** The class system is North American in origin but widely recognized; most international suppliers can map their grades to it. Composition and certificate still beat the label.

**What class is chromium copper?** It falls among the wrought copper-alloy classes (Class 2 in common supplier usage) — see our [chromium copper comparison](/guides/tungsten-copper-vs-chromium-copper/) for when the copper-alloy family stops being enough.
