A tungsten copper electrode that passes your receiving dock without inspection is a warranty on the supplier's paperwork, not on the part. Most W/Cu shipments are sound — but the failures that slip through, a soft batch, an out-of-round bore, a profile cut to the wrong revision, cost far more on the line than they would have cost at the dock. This checklist covers what a buyer should actually check on incoming W/Cu electrode wheels and machined parts, and what documentation should arrive with the box.

## Before opening: the paperwork

Check the shipment against the order before touching a part:

- **Batch traceability record** — every batch should be checked for composition, density and hardness at the factory. If your quality system requires dimensional records per wheel — bore, OD, runout, thickness against your drawing revision — those ship on request; make sure the request was in the order.
- **External certificates** — third-party stamped test reports per ASTM B702 are typically available when your system needs an external certificate rather than a factory report. These must be requested in the RFQ, not after arrival.
- **Drawing revision match** — confirm the shipment was made to the revision you last approved. A repeat order machined "to the same program" is only correct if the program matches the current revision.

## Physical checks on arrival

W/Cu is dense — typically around 13.6–15.2 g/cm³ depending on grade — so packaging failures show up as dings and edge damage. Inspect before stocking:

1. **Packaging condition.** Vacuum bags with foam lining, rods in cartons, blocks in wooden crates. A crushed corner of the crate deserves a photo before you sign.
2. **Visible damage** on faces, threads and profile edges. Surface oxidation of the copper phase is cosmetic and normal; dented profile edges are not.
3. **Weight sanity check.** A wheel noticeably light against the invoice weight is the cheapest density alarm you will ever get — a low-density batch means incomplete infiltration and short electrode life.

## The five measurable checks

These are the dimensions and properties that decide whether the part works. A workshop with calipers, a dial indicator, a bench and a hardness tester can do all of them:

- **Density.** Typically 14.2–14.8 g/cm³ for W/Cu 75/25 and 13.6–14.0 g/cm³ for 70/30. Water-displacement measurement on a sample part confirms infiltration quality; certify against the batch record rather than testing every part.
- **Electrical conductivity.** Typically ≥38% IACS for 75/25 and ≥42% IACS for 70/30. A conductivity meter on the face catches a wrong-grade shipment instantly — the most common mix-up is 75/25 supplied against a 70/30 order.
- **Hardness.** Typically 85–95 HRB (75/25) and 80–90 HRB (70/30); machinable-wheel specs are often quoted around 175–195 HB. A soft batch dresses fast and drifts out of profile — the failure that looks like "bad electrodes" three weeks later.
- **Bore and keyway.** H7 is the typical bore tolerance. Gauge the bore and slide the wheel onto a master shaft or the actual arbor: a bore that needs force or has play will hunt on the seam and wear the shaft.
- **Runout and profile.** Mount the wheel and check runout with a dial indicator — typically 0.02 mm is the specification that keeps the wheel tracking instead of chattering. Verify the edge profile (flat, bevel, R or knurled) against the drawing revision; this is the check most often skipped and most often wrong after a revision change.

For threaded electrodes, add go/no-go thread gauges to both the bore and thread checks — a thread that gauges true on arrival is the only one that screws into the gun the first time.

## When to reject

Reject, and say why in writing, when: density or conductivity falls outside the batch record; hardness is materially below the typical band; bore, runout or profile exceeds the drawing; or the drawing revision is wrong. A supplier confident in its floor will want the failed parts back or at least the gauge data — that conversation improves the next batch.
