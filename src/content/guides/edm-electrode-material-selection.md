In die-sinking EDM the electrode is not just a tool — it is a mirror that the workpiece eventually wears. Every micron of electrode wear becomes a micron of contour error in the cavity, which is why electrode material selection is an economic decision before it is a technical one. This guide places tungsten copper against graphite and pure copper, and shows where W/Cu is the material that finishes the job.

## Wear rate decides electrode economics

The cost structure of die-sinking EDM is dominated by electrodes, not sparks. An electrode wears into the negative of the cavity; finishing passes need a fresh face or a fresh electrode; and each additional electrode copy per cavity is machining, inspection, and changeover time. Cut electrode wear and you cut the number of electrode copies per job — directly.

That is what the tungsten skeleton in W/Cu buys. The rigid, sintered tungsten matrix holds fine features — sharp corners, thin ribs, small holes — through long burns where softer materials round over or shed. The copper phase keeps the electrode conductive enough that the burn stays efficient rather than merely survivable.

## Against graphite and pure copper

Each of the three common electrode materials fails somewhere, and the failures define W/Cu's position:

- **Graphite** is cheap, fast, and easy to machine — the default for roughing. But it cannot hold a knife edge, its wear ratio on carbide work is poor, and it sheds particles into the gap, which complicates flushing in fine work.
- **Pure copper** conducts beautifully and produces excellent surface finish, but it wears too fast for fine finishing and machines poorly in small sections — thin ribs and small holes in copper are a machining problem before the first spark.
- **Tungsten copper** holds detail like graphite wishes it could and survives burns that copper cannot. Its weakness is simply cost per volume, which is why standard die-shop practice uses graphite or copper for roughing and reserves W/Cu for the finishing electrodes that set final contour and corner radii.

W/Cu is not a replacement for the other two. It is the material you reach for where their specific failure modes would end up on the workpiece.

## The three places W/Cu wins outright

**Fine holes.** Small-diameter electrodes in graphite or copper lose profile faster than the hole deepens; tungsten copper keeps the electrode geometry essentially constant through the burn, so hole diameter and taper stay in tolerance without redress cycles.

**Deep, narrow features.** The classic hard case in die sinking: a deep rib or slot where flushing is poor and every wear particle works against you. W/Cu's low wear keeps the gap geometry stable, and it is often the only material that finishes such a burn without pulling the electrode for redressing.

**Low-loss finishing.** On finishing passes the electrode sets the final contour; wear here is contour error. Running W/Cu finishing electrodes in low-wear parameter regimes lets shops hold corner radii and sharp edges that no softer electrode material can deliver in hardened tool steels and carbides.

## Choosing the grade

We supply both workhorse compositions as electrode stock:

- **W/Cu 75/25 (≈ 195 HB)** — the default for electrodes where edge retention through long burns matters most: fine ribs, sharp corners, deep-hole work.
- **W/Cu 70/30 (≈ 42% IACS)** — the choice when burn efficiency matters more than maximum edge life: better conductivity, faster material removal, typically used on larger finishing electrodes and cavity work.

Both grades machine like a hard bronze on conventional tooling — carbide tooling, positive rake, flood coolant — so electrode blanks can be turned, milled and drilled without grinding, which is precisely what pure tungsten cannot offer.
