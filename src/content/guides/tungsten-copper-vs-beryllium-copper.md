Beryllium copper earned its place in resistance welding honestly: it is one of the strongest conductive copper alloys available, and shops have run it for decades. But any honest comparison today has two columns — performance, and the cost of handling a material whose dust and fumes are regulated as a serious health hazard. This guide covers both, because on long campaigns the second column often decides.

## Performance: closer than the price tags suggest

**Beryllium copper** (typically the cobalt-beryllium grade sold as RWMA Class 3) combines high strength with conductivity typically in the mid-range of copper alloys — commonly quoted around 45–55% IACS. It resists softening better than chromium copper and recovers its full strength through heat treatment.

**Tungsten copper** (W/Cu 75/25 or 70/30) sits nearby in conductivity — 38–45% IACS in our two grades — but its wear mechanism is entirely different. It is a composite: tungsten skeleton for hardness and erosion resistance, copper phase for conduction. It does not rely on a heat-treatable strengthening mechanism, so it keeps working hardness to roughly 900 °C, far beyond any wrought copper alloy.

| Property (typical) | Beryllium copper (Class 3) | W/Cu 75/25 | W/Cu 70/30 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Conductivity | ~45–55% IACS | ≥ 38% IACS | ≥ 42% IACS |
| Strength/hardness | High, heat-treatable | High, inherent | High, inherent |
| Softening behaviour | Softens well below CuW | Retains hardness to ~900 °C | Retains hardness to ~900 °C |
| Coating pickup resistance | Moderate | High | High |
| Machining dust hazard | Regulated | None special | None special |

## Where beryllium copper is still a sound choice

To be clear: for **general spot welding tips and holders on bare steel**, Class 3 remains a legitimate, widely used material. It machines well, it is cheaper per kilogram, and it is approved for exactly this work — provided the shop manages exposure. If your consumption is modest and your controls are in place, there is no performance emergency here.

## Where tungsten copper pulls ahead

- **Seam welding wheels and long-campaign electrodes** — continuous heat input works the face far above where any precipitation-strengthened alloy stays hard. See [seam welding wheel applications](/applications/seam-welding-wheels/).
- **Galvanized and coated strip** — the hard tungsten skeleton resists zinc pickup and glazing mechanically. This is the single most common reason lines migrate away from softer copper alloys, beryllium copper included.
- **High-force facings on high-strength steel** — where face growth, not heat, is the failure mode. See [resistance welding electrodes](/applications/resistance-welding-electrodes/).

## The second column: beryllium is a regulated material

The engineering community does not treat beryllium casually, and neither do regulators. Machining, welding, or grinding beryllium copper can release beryllium-containing dust and fume; beryllium exposure is associated with chronic beryllium disease, and beryllium is classified as a human carcinogen by leading health agencies. Occupational limits in the US and EU are set at very low levels compared with ordinary copper alloys.

The practical consequences for a welding operation are real, even if your exposure is modest:

1. **Air monitoring and exposure control** around any machining or dressing that generates particles.
2. **Documented controls** — ventilation, respiratory protection programs, housekeeping procedures that treat dust as hazardous waste.
3. **Supplier and disposal paperwork**, and scrutiny in customer audits — automotive and food-can OEMs increasingly ask what is in the consumables running on their lines.
4. **A compliance tail that follows the material** through regrinding, rework, and scrap.

None of this is an argument that beryllium copper cannot be used safely. It is an argument that its sticker price understates its operating cost — and that comparison belongs in the material decision.

## The switch, practically

Tungsten copper delivers the same job profile — hard, conductive enough, thermally stable — with no special-hazard classification in machining or use. Standard shop practice applies: carbide tooling, flood coolant, ordinary chips. If your weld schedule was tuned on Class 3, expect a modest current adjustment for conductivity and then a wear comparison on your own parts — that trial, run on one station, settles the question with data instead of datasheets.

## Common questions

**Does CuW conduct well enough to replace Class 3?** Usually yes, with a schedule adjustment — 70/30 at roughly 42% IACS is close to the lower end of beryllium copper grades.

**Is CuW harder to machine?** It machines like a hard bronze with carbide tooling — no grinding required, and no hazardous-dust program required either.
