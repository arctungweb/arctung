Pure tungsten and tungsten copper both belong in a modern welding shop — but in different places. Specifying pure tungsten where a CuW alloy belongs is one of the most common (and most expensive) material mistakes in resistance welding. This guide explains the physics behind the difference and gives you a simple decision rule.

## The core physics

**Pure tungsten (99.9% W)** is a refractory metal: it keeps strength at temperatures where copper electrodes would melt. But it conducts only about **30% IACS**, and its high modulus plus brittleness mean it is vulnerable to **thermal fatigue cracking** — repeated heating and cooling cycles open up grain-boundary cracks, and particles of tungsten then spall off onto the weld.

**Tungsten copper (W/Cu 75/25 or 70/30)** is a composite, not an alloy in the strict sense: a rigid, sintered tungsten skeleton infiltrated with copper. The tungsten skeleton provides hardness and erosion resistance; the copper phase does two jobs the pure metal cannot:

1. **Conducts the welding current** — 38–45% IACS, meaning less resistive heating in the electrode face for the same schedule.
2. **Pumps heat out** — the copper continuously conducts welding heat away to the cooled shank, so the face runs hundreds of degrees cooler in duty-cycle service.

The failure mode changes accordingly: instead of cracking and spalling, a CuW electrode's face slowly **dresses and re-trues** — a maintenance event, not a scrap event.

## Side-by-side

| Property (typical) | Pure W | W/Cu 75/25 | W/Cu 70/30 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Density | 19.3 g/cm³ | ~14.5 g/cm³ | ~13.8 g/cm³ |
| Conductivity | ~30% IACS | ≥ 38% IACS | ≥ 42% IACS |
| Hardness | 60–70 HRC | 85–95 HRB | 80–90 HRB |
| Thermal cycling behaviour | Cracks, spalls | Stable face | Stable face |
| Typical duty | TIG/PLASMA tips, high-temp fixtures | Resistance welding electrodes & wheels | High-current projection welding, heat sinks |

## Where pure tungsten belongs

- **TIG and plasma electrodes** — the arc runs on the tungsten itself; here its refractoriness is the point.
- **High-temperature fixtures and supports** inside furnaces, where there is no current to conduct.
- **Resistance-brazing dies** for very high-temperature work where even CuW softening is a concern.

## Where tungsten copper belongs

- **Seam welding wheels** — the copper phase keeps the face cool across millions of inches of seam. See [seam welding wheel applications](/applications/seam-welding-wheels/).
- **Spot and projection welding electrodes** — current density plus thermal cycling is exactly the job CuW was designed for. More on [resistance welding electrodes](/applications/resistance-welding-electrodes/).
- **EDM electrodes** — pure tungsten erodes slowly but machines poorly; CuW combines acceptable erosion with real manufacturability. See [EDM electrode applications](/applications/edm-electrodes/).
- **Heat sinks and electrical contacts** where thermal resistance matters as much as strength — see [heat sink applications](/applications/heat-sinks-contacts/).

## The decision rule

Ask one question: **does the electrode carry welding current while being thermally cycled?**

- Yes → tungsten copper. Pure tungsten's brittleness and low conductivity will cost you electrode life and weld consistency.
- No → pure tungsten may be right; it is simply not a product we manufacture.

## Common questions

**Is CuW harder to machine than pure tungsten?** The opposite. Pure tungsten is notorious — it is machined by grinding and EDM more than by cutting. Infiltrated CuW cuts like a hard bronze on conventional tooling.

**Does the copper melt out at welding temperatures?** No. The copper is held in a continuous tungsten skeleton; at 900 °C the composite still retains useful hardness, which is why CuW is specified against softening rather than melting.
